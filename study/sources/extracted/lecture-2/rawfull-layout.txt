 Why Do Traders Use Derivatives?


  Three important motives
      Hedging: reducing an existing exposure to risk
          Speculation: taking risk in anticipation of a price movement
          Arbitrage: exploiting inconsistent prices in different markets

  Important distinction
  These categories describe a trader’s objective, not the instrument being
  traded. The same derivative may be used for hedging, speculation, or
  arbitrage.

 Can the Same Share Have Two Different Prices?
  Suppose the following prices are observed simultaneously:
      London share price: $ 100
      New York share price: $150
      Exchange rate: 1.5300 USD per GBP
  Compare prices in the same currency

                                            $ 100 × 1.5300 = $153.
  The share is $3 cheaper in New York.

  Arbitrage strategy
  Buy the share in New York for $150 and simultaneously sell it in London
  for the equivalent of $153:

                                 profit per share = $153 − $150 = $3.

  Ignoring transaction costs, taxes, bid–ask spreads, trading restrictions, and settlement

 How Much Protection Does the Put Provide?

  At maturity, the value of the hedged position is

                            VT = 1,000ST + 1,000 max(27.50 − ST , 0).

                          ST          Share value        Put payoff          Hedged value
                       $20.00               $20,000            $7,500            $27,500

  The put establishes a minimum terminal value of $27,500. Protection is
  not free: the investor paid a $1,000 premium initially.

 Long and Short Futures Hedges

  Long hedge
  Use a long futures position when an asset will be purchased later and the
  hedger is concerned that its price may rise.

  Short hedge
  Use a short futures position when an asset will be sold later and the
  hedger is concerned that its price may fall.

  Classify each exposure
      1   An airline will buy fuel in three months.
      2   A farmer will sell wheat after the harvest.
      3   A UK importer must buy US dollars in two months.
      4   A fund plans to sell part of its equity portfolio.

 Short Hedge for the Sale of an Asset

  A hedger will sell an asset at t2 and shorts futures at t1 . Let F1 be the
  initial futures price, and let S2 and F2 be the spot and futures prices when
  the hedge is closed.
  Effective selling price
  The asset is sold for S2 , and the short futures position earns F1 − F2 .
  Therefore,

                          S2 + (F1 − F2 ) = F1 + b2 ,                      b2 = S2 − F2 .

  Interpretation
  If b2 were known when the hedge began, the effective selling price would
  be known. Uncertainty about b2 creates basis risk.

 Long Hedge for the Purchase of an Asset


  A hedger will buy an asset at t2 and buys futures at t1 .
  Effective purchase price
  The asset costs S2 , and the long futures position earns F2 − F1 .
  Subtracting the futures gain from the cash purchase price gives

                          S2 − (F2 − F1 ) = F1 + b2 ,                      b2 = S2 − F2 .

  Key point
  Long and short hedges produce the same algebraic effective price, F1 + b2 ,
  but they protect opposite commercial exposures.

 A Numerical Example of Basis Risk

  A producer shorts futures at F1 = $52. When the hedge is closed,

                                            S2 = $47,            F2 = $48.

  Effective selling price
  The cash-market sale produces $47 and the futures gain is

                                            F1 − F2 = 52 − 48 = $4.

  Hence,
                                 effective selling price = 47 + 4 = $51.
  Equivalently,
                                    F1 + b2 = 52 + (47 − 48) = $51.

 Understanding Basis Risk



  The basis is
                                               bt = St − Ft .

          The basis may strengthen or weaken while the hedge is open.
          The closing basis determines the effective hedged price.
          For the same underlying and delivery terms, spot and futures prices
          converge at delivery, so bT = 0.
          Cross hedges generally retain basis risk because the exposure and
          futures contract reference different assets.

 Choosing a Futures Contract


      1   Choose an underlying whose price is closely related to the exposure.
      2   Choose a delivery month close to, but normally later than, the end of
          the hedge.
      3   Check the contract size and calculate the required number of
          contracts.
      4   Consider liquidity, bid–ask spreads, and basis risk.

  Cross hedge
  When no futures contract exists on the asset being hedged, use a futures
  contract on a related asset whose price changes are strongly correlated
  with the exposure.

 Deriving the Minimum-Variance Hedge Ratio

  For a hedged exposure, write the change in value per unit as

                                            ∆VH = ∆S − h∆F .

  Its variance is
                                 Var(∆VH ) = σS2 + h2 σF2 − 2hρσS σF .
  Minimising with respect to h,

                                 ∂ Var(∆VH )
                                             = 2hσF2 − 2ρσS σF = 0.
                                     ∂h
  Therefore,
                                                             σS
                                                  h∗ = ρ        .
                                                             σF

 Interpreting the Optimal Hedge Ratio

  In
                                                              σS
                                                   h∗ = ρ        ,
                                                              σF
  σS is the standard deviation of spot-price changes, σF is the standard
  deviation of futures-price changes, and ρ is their correlation.
          A higher correlation generally makes the hedge more effective.
          h∗ need not equal one.
          The hedge minimises variance; it does not necessarily eliminate risk.

  Regression interpretation
  In the regression
                                            ∆St = α + h∆Ft + εt ,
  the estimated slope is the minimum-variance hedge ratio.

  If QA is the size of the exposure and QF is the quantity covered by one
  futures contract, then
                                           QA
                                  N ∗ = h∗    .

  The futures position covers the equivalent of

                                              N ∗ QF = h∗ QA

 Stock Indices and Index Futures

  A stock index measures the performance of a specified portfolio of shares.
  Different indices use different weighting rules:
          S&P 500: float-adjusted market-capitalisation weighted
          Nasdaq-100: modified market-capitalisation weighted
          Dow Jones Industrial Average: price weighted

  CME S&P 500 futures examples
          E-mini S&P 500: $50× the index
          Micro E-mini S&P 500: $5× the index

  For an index level of 5,000, the respective contract notionals are $250,000
  and $25,000.

 Market Risk and Beta

  A portfolio does not normally mirror an index exactly. Its sensitivity to
  market movements can be represented by beta:

                                    rP − rf = α + βP (rM − rf ) + εP .

          βP : exposure to systematic market risk
          εP : idiosyncratic return not explained by the market
          βP = 1: portfolio tends to move with the market
          βP > 1: greater sensitivity to market movements

  Limitation
  Beta is estimated from historical data and may change. Index futures
  hedge systematic risk, not the portfolio’s idiosyncratic risk.

          If βP < β ∗ , buy futures.

 Why Use a Beta Hedge?


  An investor may have a view about particular shares but not about the
  overall market.
  Example
  An investor expects a portfolio of biopharmaceutical shares to outperform
  the market but is concerned that the entire market may fall. Short index
  futures can reduce the broad market exposure while retaining the
  portfolio’s relative performance exposure.

  What remains after the hedge?
  The hedge reduces estimated systematic risk. Sector-specific,
  company-specific, basis, model, and estimation risks remain.

 Trading Controls and Rogue-Trader Risk

          A hedge can become a speculative position if its size or purpose
          changes.
          Trading mandates should define permitted instruments, position
          limits, and hedge objectives.
          Independent valuation, reporting, and reconciliation help detect
          unauthorised risk-taking.
          Managers should compare the derivative position with the exposure it
          is intended to hedge.

  Final control question
  What exposure is this position hedging, and does its size match that
  exposure?

 Lecture Summary


          Hedgers reduce risk; speculators deliberately take risk; arbitrageurs
          exploit price inconsistencies.
          A long futures hedge protects a future purchase; a short hedge
          protects a future sale.
          Basis risk arises because the closing basis is uncertain.
          The minimum-variance hedge ratio is h∗ = ρσS /σF .
          Index futures can change a portfolio’s systematic market exposure.

  Core principle
  A hedge should be evaluated by how it changes the risk of the complete
  position, not by whether the derivative makes a profit by itself.
