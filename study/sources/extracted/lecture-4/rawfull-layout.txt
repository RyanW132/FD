 Investment and Consumption Assets

  Investment assets
  Assets held by significant numbers of investors primarily for investment,
  such as shares, bonds, gold, and silver.

  Consumption assets
  Assets held mainly for use or consumption, such as oil, copper, wheat, and
  cocoa.

  Why the distinction matters
  For an investment asset, cash-and-carry arbitrage often enforces a precise
  relationship between spot and forward prices. For a consumption asset,
  inventory may provide operational benefits, so the relationship may involve
  a convenience yield and need not be enforced by the same arbitrage
  strategy.

 Why Trade Forward or Futures Contracts?


  Forward and futures contracts allow market participants to transfer price
  risk across time.
          A hedger can lock in a future purchase or sale price.
          A speculator can take a view on a future price without initially
          purchasing the underlying asset.
          An arbitrageur can exploit inconsistent spot and derivative prices.
          Observable futures prices can contribute to price discovery.

  Important distinction
  A futures price reflects today’s market-clearing price for future delivery. It
  is not necessarily an unbiased forecast of the future spot price.

 Why Is Short Selling Important?
  Short selling allows investors to benefit from, or hedge against, falling
  prices.
          Shares are borrowed and sold in the market.
          The short seller later repurchases the shares and returns them.
          The short seller must compensate the lender for dividends and other
          distributions.
          Borrowing fees, collateral requirements, and recall risk may apply.
          A short position can suffer an unlimited loss if the asset price rises
          without bound.
  Pricing relevance
  Some no-arbitrage arguments require the ability to short the underlying
  asset. If short selling is restricted, only one side of the pricing relationship
  may be enforceable.

 Daily Settlement of Futures
  For N long contracts, each covering Q units, the daily gain is

                                            ∆Vt = NQ(Ft − Ft−1 ).

          Initial margin is deposited when the position is opened.
          Daily gains and losses enter the margin account through variation
          margin.
          If the balance falls below the maintenance margin, a margin call may
          require the account to be replenished.

  Example
  Two long contracts cover 100 units each. If the futures price falls from
  $52 to $50.50,

                              ∆V = 2 × 100 × (50.50 − 52) = −$300.

 Pricing Assumptions

  The basic model assumes that:
          there are no transaction costs or taxes;
          assets can be traded in divisible quantities;
          market participants can borrow and lend at the relevant risk-free rate;
          short selling is possible when required;
          contractual cash flows and carrying costs are known; and
          arbitrage opportunities are exploited.

  Role of assumptions
  The formulas describe theoretical no-arbitrage benchmarks. Funding
  spreads, bid-ask spreads, collateral, taxes, and short-sale constraints can
  create a range around the benchmark in real markets.

 Notation

          St : spot price of the underlying at time t
          Ft,T : forward price at time t for delivery at T
          K : delivery price written into an existing contract
          T − t: time remaining to delivery
          r : continuously compounded risk-free rate for the remaining term
          I : present value of known cash income before delivery
          q: continuously compounded income yield
          u: proportional storage-cost rate
          y : convenience yield

  At inception
  For a fairly priced new forward contract, K = F0,T and its value is zero.
  After inception, K is fixed but the market forward price changes.

 Forward Price with No Income

  Consider an investment asset that pays no income before delivery.
  Cash-and-carry strategy
  At time 0:
      1   borrow S0 ;
      2   buy one unit of the asset; and
      3   short a forward contract for delivery at T .
  At T , deliver the asset for F0,T and repay S0 e rT .

  No arbitrage requires
                                                   F0,T = S0 e rT .
  This is a cost-of-carry result, not a forecast of ST .

 If the Market Forward Price Is Too High
  Suppose S0 = $40, r = 5%, T = 0.25, and the quoted forward price is $43.
  The no-arbitrage price is
                                        ∗
                                       F0,T = 40e 0.05×0.25 = $40.50.


  Cash-and-carry arbitrage
      1   Borrow $40 and buy the share.
      2   Short the forward at $43.
      3   At maturity, deliver the share and receive $43.
      4   Repay $40.50.

  The locked-in profit at T is

                                            $43.00 − $40.50 = $2.50 .

 If the Market Forward Price Is Too Low

  Suppose instead that the quoted forward price is $39.
  Reverse cash-and-carry arbitrage
      1   Short one share and receive $40.
      2   Invest $40 for three months.
      3   Enter a long forward contract at $39.
      4   At maturity, the investment is worth $40.50.
      5   Pay $39 for the share and return it to the lender.

  The locked-in profit at T is

                                            $40.50 − $39.00 = $1.50 ,

  before stock-borrowing costs.

 What If Short Selling Is Restricted?
  Cash-and-carry can still rule out a forward price that is too high:
                                                   F0,T ≤ S0 e rT
  under the basic no-income assumptions.
  Ruling out a forward price that is too low requires the reverse
  cash-and-carry strategy, which normally involves shorting the asset.
  Consequences
  If investors cannot short the asset and do not already own it, the
  lower-bound arbitrage may be unavailable. The equality

                                                   F0,T = S0 e rT

  is therefore not guaranteed by that argument alone.

  For an investment asset held by many investors, existing owners may still
  sell the asset and buy it forward, helping enforce the relationship.

 Forward Price with Known Cash Income
  Suppose the asset pays known cash income before delivery with present
  value I . Owning the asset provides both the terminal asset and the interim
  income.
  The prepaid cost of the terminal asset is therefore S0 − I , giving

                                              F0,T = (S0 − I )e rT .

  Known income reduces the forward price because the forward holder does

 Known-Income Example

  A bond is priced at $900. It will pay a $40 coupon after four months. A
  nine-month forward is considered. The four-month zero rate is 3%, and
  the nine-month zero rate is 4%, both continuously compounded.
  The coupon’s present value is

                                        I = 40e −0.03·(4/12) = $39.60.

  Therefore,
                                     F0,T = (900 − 39.60)e 0.04·(9/12)
                                             = $886.60 .

  Check
  Why is the forward price below the spot price even though interest rates
  are positive?

 Forward Price with a Known Yield

  If the asset provides a continuously compounded income yield q, the
  no-arbitrage forward price is

                                                F0,T = S0 e (r −q)T .


  Example
  Let S0 = 25, r = 10%, q = 3.96%, and T = 0.5. Then

                                F0,T = 25e (0.10−0.0396)×0.5 = $25.77 .

  Cash income versus yield
  Use (S0 − I )e rT for known cash amounts and S0 e (r −q)T for income
  proportional to the asset’s value.

 Forward Price Is Not Forward Value


  At inception, a fairly priced forward has

                                       K = F0,T                  and           f0 = 0.

  At a later time t:
          the contractual delivery price K remains fixed;
          the market forward price becomes Ft,T ; and
          the contract value ft is generally no longer zero.
  For a long forward,
                                            ft = (Ft,T − K )e −r (T −t) .
  For a short forward, the value is −ft .

 Deriving the Value of a Long Forward
  Construct a portfolio at time t:
      1   Keep the existing long forward with delivery price K .
      2   Enter a new short forward with delivery price Ft,T . A new fairly
          priced forward has zero value.
  At delivery, the combined payoff is certain:

                                 (ST − K ) + (Ft,T − ST ) = Ft,T − K .

  Discounting this certain payment gives

                                            ft = (Ft,T − K )e −r (T −t) .

  For a no-income asset, substituting Ft,T = St e r (T −t) gives the equivalent
  expression
                                              ft = St − Ke −r (T −t) .

 Valuing an Existing Forward: Example

  A long forward on a non-dividend-paying share has delivery price K = $24.
  Six months remain. The current share price is $25 and the continuously
  compounded risk-free rate is 10%.
  The current forward price is

                                        Ft,T = 25e 0.10×0.5 = $26.28.

  The value of the existing long forward is

                                            ft = (26.28 − 24)e −0.10×0.5
                                              = $2.17 .

  Equivalently,
                                     ft = 25 − 24e −0.10×0.5 = $2.17.

 Forward and Futures Prices

  Forward and futures prices are equal when interest rates are deterministic
  and the contracts otherwise have the same underlying and delivery date.
  When rates are uncertain, daily settlement can create a difference:
          If futures gains tend to occur when interest rates are high, those gains
          can be reinvested at favourable rates, tending to make the futures
          price exceed the forward price.
          If futures gains tend to occur when rates are low, the opposite
          tendency arises.

  Key mechanism
  The difference comes from the timing and reinvestment of daily
  variation-margin cash flows, not from a different terminal payoff formula.

 Stock-Index Futures
  A stock index can be treated as an investment asset paying a dividend
  yield q. Under the standard assumptions,

                                            F0,T = S0 e (r −q)T .


  Example
  For a three-month contract, let

                         S0 = 1,300,        r = 5%,          q = 1%,      T = 0.25.

  Then
                             F0,T = 1,300e (0.05−0.01)×0.25 = 1,313.07 .

  The dividend yield used should correspond to dividends expected during
  the life of the contract.

 Currency Forwards: Covered Interest Parity

  Let S0 be the domestic-currency price of one unit of foreign currency.
  Holding foreign currency allows the investor to earn the foreign interest
  rate rf , while domestic funds earn rd .
  No arbitrage therefore requires

                                            F0,T = S0 e (rd −rf )T .


  Quotation matters
  The signs depend on how the exchange rate is quoted. Here both S0 and
  F0,T are domestic currency per unit of foreign currency.

  If rd < rf , the foreign currency trades at a forward discount under this
  quotation convention.

 Currency Arbitrage When the Forward Is Too Low


  If the two-year forward rate is 0.9300 USD per AUD:
      1   Borrow 1,000 AUD and convert it to 980 USD.
      2   Invest 980 USD at 1% for two years, producing 980e 0.01×2 = $999.80.
      3   The AUD loan grows to 1,000e 0.03×2 = 1,061.84 AUD.
      4   Buy 1,061.84 AUD forward for 1,061.84 × 0.9300 = $987.51.
      5   Use the AUD to repay the loan.
  The riskless profit at maturity is

                                      $999.80 − $987.51 = $12.29 .

 Commodities: Storage Costs and Convenience Yield
  Physical commodities may involve:
        financing cost r ;
        storage and insurance costs u; and
        convenience yield y , the non-cash benefit of holding inventory.
  If proportional carry components are used,

                                            F0,T = S0 e (r +u−y )T .
  If storage costs are known cash amounts with present value U, and there is
  no convenience yield,
                                            F0,T = (S0 + U)e rT .

  Consumption assets
  Convenience yield can make holding physical inventory more valuable than
  holding only a forward contract. Reverse cash-and-carry arbitrage may
  therefore be unavailable.

 The Cost of Carry
  The net proportional cost of carrying an asset to delivery is
                                            c = r + u − q − y,
  where terms that do not apply are set to zero. Then
                                              F0,T = S0 e cT .

                 Underlying                                               Net cost of carry
                 Non-dividend-paying investment asset                             r
                 Stock index                                                    r −q
                 Foreign currency                                              rd − rf
                 Commodity                                                   r +u−y

  Interpret carefully
  Cost of carry explains the spot-forward relationship; it does not imply that
  the future spot price must equal today’s forward price.

 Commodity Forward: Known Storage Cost

  Consider a one-year contract on an investment asset with:

                                            S0 = $450,              r = 7%,

  and a $2 storage payment due at the end of the year.
  The present value of the storage cost is

                                             U = 2e −0.07 = $1.865.

  Therefore,
                                            F0,T = (450 + 1.865)e 0.07
                                                 = $484.63 .
  If the quoted forward price is above $484.63, cash-and-carry arbitrage is
  possible under the model assumptions.

 Contango and Backwardation


       Contango The forward price exceeds the spot price.
  Backwardation The forward price is below the spot price.
  From
                                            F0,T = S0 e (r +u−q−y )T ,
  contango is associated with positive net carry, while a sufficiently large
  income or convenience yield can produce backwardation.
  Do not infer too much
  Contango does not by itself mean that the market expects the spot price
  to rise, and backwardation does not by itself mean that the market expects
  it to fall. Carry and risk premia also matter.

 Lecture Summary
          Forward prices follow from spot prices and the net cost of carry.
          Cash-and-carry addresses an overpriced forward; reverse
          cash-and-carry addresses an underpriced forward when short selling is
          possible.
          Known cash income and proportional yields require different formulas.
          The value of an existing forward is not the same as its current forward
          price.
          Daily settlement can create a difference between futures and forward
          prices when interest rates are uncertain.
          Currency pricing follows covered interest parity.
          Commodity pricing may include storage costs and convenience yield.
  Unifying principle
  Replicate future delivery using spot-market borrowing, lending, ownership,
  and income. If the derivative price differs from the replication cost,
  investigate the arbitrage and the assumptions it requires.
