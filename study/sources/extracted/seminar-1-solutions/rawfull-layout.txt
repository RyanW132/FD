Question 1
Give an example of a hedger, a speculator, and an arbitrageur in derivatives markets.
   Why might a hedger sometimes be accused of ”speculating”? What controls should firms
have in place?

Solution
    Hedger: A wheat farmer expects to sell wheat in three months and is concerned that
     its price may fall. The farmer shorts wheat futures.
     If wheat prices fall, the gain on the futures position offsets some or all of the lower
     cash-market revenue. The hedge stabilises the selling price, although basis risk may
     remain.

    Speculator: A trader expects crude-oil prices to rise and buys oil futures without
     having an existing oil exposure.
     The trader profits if the futures price rises and loses if it falls. The objective is to take
     risk in anticipation of a return.

    Arbitrageur: Suppose gold is priced at $2,000 per ounce and carrying costs imply a
     fair one-year futures price of $2,120, but the market futures price is $2,150.
     The arbitrageur can buy gold, finance and carry it for one year, and short the futures
     contract. At maturity, the gold is delivered for $2,150, generating an arbitrage profit
     of

                                       $2,150 − $2,120 = $30

     per ounce, before transaction costs.

   A hedge may become speculative if the derivative position does not match the underlying
exposure. This can occur when the firm:

    hedges more than its actual exposure;


                                               1
    trades without an underlying exposure;

    changes the hedge according to market forecasts; or

    uses instruments that introduce additional risks.

   Firms should therefore have:

    a written hedging policy;

    documentation linking each derivative to an underlying exposure;

    position limits and appropriate authorisation;

    independent risk monitoring and valuation; and

    regular reporting and reconciliation.

   A hedge should be evaluated by how effectively it reduces the risk of the combined
position, not by whether the derivative makes a profit.


Question 2
Which of the following instruments are OTC vs exchange-traded?
    Futures on crude oil

    A customized forward contract on jet fuel

    An S&P 500 index option

    A 5-year interest rate swap

    What are the advantages and disadvantages of OTC vs exchange? Why do most FX and
interest rate derivatives trade OTC instead of on exchanges?

Solution
                   Instrument                         Typical market
                   Crude-oil futures             Exchange-traded
                   Customised jet-fuel forward   OTC
                   Standard S&P 500 index option Exchange-traded
                   Five-year interest-rate swap  OTC

  Exchange-traded derivatives have standardised contract sizes, maturities, and terms.
Their main advantages are:

    transparent market prices;

                                              2
    generally high liquidity;

    central clearing and daily margining; and

    reduced and actively managed counterparty risk.

  Their main disadvantage is limited flexibility: the available contracts may not match the
amount, maturity, or underlying exposure that a firm needs to hedge.
   OTC derivatives are negotiated between counterparties. Their main advantage is cus-
tomisation: the parties can choose the notional amount, maturity, underlying asset, and
payment terms.
   Their disadvantages may include:

    lower liquidity for customised contracts;

    less public price transparency;

    more complex documentation and valuation; and

    counterparty risk, particularly for uncleared contracts.

    Many FX and interest-rate derivatives trade OTC because firms and financial institutions
often need contracts that match precise currencies, amounts, dates, interest-rate conventions,
and cash flows. OTC dealer markets can provide this flexibility and can also be highly liquid
for commonly traded products.
    Some standardised OTC derivatives are centrally cleared, while uncleared contracts may
be subject to collateral, margin, and reporting requirements.

Question 4
Define the basis and explain why it matters in a hedge.
   Spot price of corn today = $3.00, futures price (3 months) = $3.10. In 3 months, spot =
$2.70, futures = $2.75.

    What was the basis at hedge initiation and at hedge close?

    How did the basis risk affect the hedge outcome?

   Why does the basis tend to converge to zero at maturity?

Solution
The basis is defined as

                                        bt = St − Ft ,
   where St is the spot price and Ft is the futures price.
   The initial basis is

                          b0 = S0 − F0 = 3.00 − 3.10 = −$0.10 .
   The closing basis is

                          bT = ST − FT = 2.70 − 2.75 = −$0.05 .
   The basis has strengthened, because it increased from −$0.10 to −$0.05.
   For a short hedger, such as a corn farmer, the effective selling price is

                             Effective price = ST + (F0 − FT ).
   Therefore,

                      Effective price = 2.70 + (3.10 − 2.75) = $3.05 .
   Equivalently,

                      Effective price = F0 + bT = 3.10 − 0.05 = $3.05.
   If the basis had remained at −$0.10, the effective price would have been

                                    3.10 − 0.10 = $3.00.
   Thus, the strengthening basis improves the short hedger’s effective selling price by $0.05
per bushel. For a long hedger, the same basis strengthening would increase the effective
purchase price and would therefore be unfavourable.
   The uncertainty about the closing basis is called basis risk. It means that a futures
hedge does not normally guarantee the exact initial futures price.

Question 5
An airline wants to hedge jet fuel costs, but there are no jet fuel futures. It considers using
crude oil futures.

    What are the risks in this strategy? What factors determine whether cross-hedging is
effective?

Solution
Because the airline will buy fuel in the future and is concerned that prices may rise, it should
take a long position in crude-oil futures.
   This is a cross-hedge because the asset being hedged, jet fuel, is different from the asset
underlying the futures contract, crude oil.
   The main risks are:

    Imperfect correlation: jet-fuel and crude-oil prices may not move together.

    Refining-spread risk: changes in refining costs and margins can cause jet-fuel prices
     to move differently from crude-oil prices.

    Timing mismatch: the futures maturity may not coincide with the airline’s fuel-
     purchase date.

    Location and benchmark mismatch: the crude-oil contract may refer to a different
     region or pricing benchmark.

    Margin risk: daily futures losses may create cash-flow requirements even if the phys-
     ical hedge is economically effective.

   The cross-hedge is more effective when changes in jet-fuel prices are strongly and consis-
tently related to changes in crude-oil futures prices.
   A high and stable correlation, appropriate contract maturity, similar pricing locations,
and good futures-market liquidity generally make the cross-hedge more effective. However,
because the two commodities are not identical, some basis risk remains.
